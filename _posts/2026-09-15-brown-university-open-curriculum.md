When we talk about preparing our children for college, the conversation can quickly fill with deadlines, grades, and applications. I want to leave room for another question: What kind of learning environment will help them grow?

Brown University's Open Curriculum offers an interesting starting point. College Intel's article, [What Is Brown's Open Curriculum, Really?](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/brown-open-curriculum-explained), explores the freedom students have to shape their education and the responsibility that comes with it. It is a useful read for families looking beyond a college's name to understand the experience it offers.

At Brown, students do not follow a prescribed general core or distribution requirements. They still have degree requirements: at least 30 courses, a completed concentration (Brown's term for a major), demonstrated writing competence, and the equivalent of eight full-time semesters of enrollment. Brown describes this flexibility in its [official Open Curriculum overview](https://college.brown.edu/design-your-education/explore-open-curriculum).

That combination invites an appealing possibility: a student can take an interest seriously without making it their entire academic identity. Imagine someone drawn to science who also wants to explore storytelling. I see value in making space for both, especially while a young person is still discovering the questions they care about.

There is flexibility in grading, too. Students generally choose between letter grades and Satisfactory/No Credit, although some courses require the latter. Brown does not calculate students' GPAs. Its [grade options guidance](https://college.brown.edu/design-your-education/explore-open-curriculum/course-selection/grade-options) explains the rules and recommends speaking with an academic advising dean about applications for graduate school or other external opportunities.

For me, the most interesting part is the decision-making this approach asks of a student. Choosing a course because it feels familiar is different from choosing it because it stretches your thinking. With more choice, it becomes especially useful to ask why a class belongs in your education.

As a family conversation, I would start with a few questions:

- What subject would you explore even if nobody required it?
- How would you make room for something that feels difficult or unfamiliar?
- When you feel unsure about a decision, how comfortable are you asking for help?
- Would choosing your own academic direction feel energizing, overwhelming, or a little of both?

There does not need to be a perfect answer. A teenager who wants guidance is giving us useful information about what helps them learn. A teenager eager for independence may still need practice planning ahead and listening to advice.

What I take from Brown's approach is a reason to pay closer attention to curiosity long before college. At home, that might mean letting a child choose a library book, follow a question, or explain why a topic interests them. Those small conversations give us opportunities to listen.

When the time comes to consider colleges, I hope we can carry that habit with us: asking thoughtful questions about the person our child is becoming and the environment where their curiosity can grow.
