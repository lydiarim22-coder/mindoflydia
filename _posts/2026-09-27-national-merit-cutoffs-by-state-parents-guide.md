College planning has a way of turning a perfectly ordinary evening into a research project. You look up one unfamiliar term, and suddenly you have six tabs open and another number to understand.

National Merit cutoffs can feel like that. There is a PSAT score, a Selection Index, a state cutoff, and a graduating class attached to the whole conversation. Before comparing numbers, it helps to know which question each one answers.

College Intel's [National Merit cutoff guide by state](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/national-merit-cutoffs-by-state) is a useful starting point. It brings the Class of 2027 figures together in one place. Here is how I would approach that information as a parent.

## Why National Merit cutoffs vary by state

Semifinalist selection uses state-based allocations tied to each state's share of the country's high school graduating seniors. Within a state, qualifying Selection Index scores determine which eligible entrants reach that level. That means the same score can have different outcomes in different states, and cutoffs can change between years. NMSC explains the process in its [official program-recognition guidance](https://www.nationalmerit.org/s/1758/interior.aspx?sid=1758&gid=2&pgid=1881).

The practical takeaway: start with the student's correct competition year and selection unit. If a boarding school, a move, or another unusual enrollment situation makes that unclear, ask the school counselor to help confirm it.

These are reported cutoffs for the **Class of 2027**, associated with the fall 2025 qualifying PSAT/NMSQT. They are not announced cutoffs for students taking the test in fall 2026 for the Class of 2028 competition. For other states, visit [College Intel's complete state table](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/national-merit-cutoffs-by-state).

Treat a published chart as a reference. Confirm an individual student's official standing through the school and NMSC.

## Find the Selection Index, not just the total PSAT score

The Selection Index is a separate number on the PSAT/NMSQT score report. College Board explains its calculation in the [official score-report guide](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/scores/what-scores-mean):

**Selection Index = (2 × Reading and Writing score + Math score) ÷ 10**

For example, a hypothetical student with 740 in Reading and Writing and 730 in Math would have:

**(2 × 740 + 730) ÷ 10 = 221**

Because Reading and Writing receives double weight, two students with the same total PSAT score can have different Selection Index scores. Use the index shown on the report when checking a cutoff.

I would put three things on a single page: the graduating class, the Selection Index, and the applicable state or selection unit. It is easier to have a useful conversation when everyone is looking at the same information.

## Semifinalist recognition is a step, not a scholarship offer

NMSC's [September 2026 announcement](https://www.nationalmerit.org/s/1758/blog.aspx?sid=1758&gid=2&pgid=601) explains that Semifinalists must meet additional requirements to advance to Finalist standing and compete for National Merit Scholarships.

For a student who has received that recognition, I would ask the counselor:

- What application materials and school documents are still needed?
- What are the student's deadlines, including any earlier school deadline?
- What confirming test-score requirements apply?
- Where should the family check the exact terms of any college scholarship being considered?

Keep the school's instructions and official program materials together. A score comparison cannot replace those next steps.

## Keep the college conversation bigger than one number

Recognition can be exciting. Missing a cutoff can also feel disappointing, especially when a student has worked hard. I would want to make room for either reaction before turning the conversation into another checklist.

Then I would return to the questions that matter throughout a college search: Where could this student learn well? What support would help them thrive? What would attendance actually cost our family?
