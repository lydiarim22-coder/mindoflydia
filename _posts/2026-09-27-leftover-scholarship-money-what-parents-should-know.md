A scholarship award can bring a wonderful sense of relief. After applications, essays, and all the waiting, there is finally some help with the college bill.

Then the student account shows a credit balance, or a refund arrives, and a new question appears: Can we use this money?

The answer depends on the award's terms, the school's accounting, and the expenses the money will cover. I would want to understand those details before adding the refund to the family budget.

College Intel's article, [What Happens to Leftover Scholarship Money? Can You Keep It?](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/what-to-do-with-leftover-scholarship-money), raises several useful questions about refunds and scholarship restrictions. Here is a practical way for parents and students to work through them, with official sources for the tax and FAFSA rules.

## Start with the award letter

Before making plans, find the scholarship's written terms. Look for the expenses it covers, the semester or academic year it applies to, and any instructions about unused funds.

I would ask the scholarship provider:

- Is this award limited to tuition, or can it cover other education expenses?
- Can unused money carry forward to another term?
- Does any unused amount have to be returned?
- What happens if enrollment, housing, or the student's course load changes?

If the letter does not answer a question, request clarification in writing. A short email saved alongside the award letter can make the next decision much easier.

## Ask the school to explain the balance

A refund may involve several sources of funding. Ask the financial aid office and billing office to identify what produced the credit, whether all charges have posted, and whether any aid adjustments are pending.

For a local example, the University of Maryland's [billing FAQs](https://financialaid.umd.edu/resources-policies/billing-faqs) tell students to report outside scholarships and explain its process for refunds from excess financial aid. Families at other colleges should check their own school's instructions.

A new scholarship can also affect an existing aid package. Federal aid rules include procedures for resolving awards that exceed applicable limits; the [Federal Student Aid Handbook](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/fsa-handbook/2026-2027/vol6/ch1-campus-based-programs-common-elements) describes this for campus-based programs. Ask the school to show you the revised package and explain any change to grants, loans, or work-study.

One question I would put near the top of the list: **Does any part of this refund come from a loan?** Borrowed money still comes with repayment obligations. If the student does not need it, ask about reducing or returning that portion through the appropriate process.

## Check whether the scholarship money is taxable

The IRS generally allows tax-free scholarship treatment for a degree candidate at an eligible institution when funds pay required tuition and fees or course-required books, supplies, and equipment. Amounts used for room and board, travel, or optional equipment generally must be included in income. See [IRS Topic 421 on scholarships and grants](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc421).

An award may permit a housing expense even though that use has tax consequences. Permission to spend the money and its tax treatment are separate questions.

For a hypothetical example, suppose a qualifying student receives a $4,000 scholarship that permits both tuition and housing expenses. If $3,000 pays required tuition and $1,000 pays rent, the tuition portion may qualify for tax-free treatment while the rent portion is generally taxable. That does not mean the student owes $1,000 in tax; the amount due depends on the student's full tax situation.

Keep receipts and ask a qualified tax professional about filing requirements, especially when education credits or 529 withdrawals are also involved.

## Read the FAFSA instructions before reporting cash

This is a detail worth slowing down for: a student-aid refund in a bank account does not automatically become ordinary reportable savings.

The [2026-27 FAFSA form](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/2026-27-fafsa-form.pdf), under Student Assets, instructs applicants to exclude student aid from the total for cash, savings, and checking accounts. Follow the instructions for the specific FAFSA year being completed, and ask the aid office how to document any unspent aid mixed with other funds.

Keep records showing where the money came from and how much remains. There is no need to rush into spending or transferring it simply because a FAFSA filing date is approaching.

## Give the remaining money a clear plan

Once the school and scholarship provider have confirmed what is available and permitted, make a small semester budget. List upcoming expenses, the amount the award can cover, and any deadline for using or returning funds.

I would keep the award letter, updated aid offer, student-account statement, provider emails, and receipts together. That gives the student something concrete to work from when another bill arrives.
