If you've ever tried to understand what it takes to get into a highly selective college, you know how quickly the advice can become overwhelming.

Get perfect grades.

Take the hardest classes.

Score well on the SAT.

Start a club. Volunteer. Do research. Win awards.

And somehow, find time to be a teenager.

For parents, it can feel as though every new article adds another requirement to an already crowded checklist.

But what happens when we look at actual reported admissions outcomes rather than individual success stories?

A recent College Intel analysis offers an interesting perspective.

In [Accepted vs. Rejected Applicants at Top Colleges: What 3,525 Real 2026 Decisions Show](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/accepted-vs-rejected-applicants-top-colleges-2026), College Intel examines thousands of self-reported college admissions decisions and compares characteristics of applicants who were admitted with those who were rejected or waitlisted.

The findings are worth exploring, especially for families trying to understand which parts of a college application deserve attention.

## Where the information comes from

College Intel reviewed 404 posts from Reddit's r/collegeresults community, primarily covering students entering college in fall 2026.

Its analysis includes 3,525 admissions decisions from 385 applicants across 52 selective colleges with official admission rates below 15%.

Those schools include Ivy League universities, Stanford, MIT, Duke, and several other competitive institutions.

But there is an important detail to understand before looking at the numbers.

This is not a representative sample of all college applicants.

The students voluntarily shared their own results online. Their grades, scores, activities, and admissions decisions were not independently verified.

And the group was unusually strong academically.

The sample's overall admission rate was approximately 29%, compared with an average official admission rate of about 7.7% at the colleges represented.

That doesn't mean the typical applicant has a 29% chance of admission.

It means the students who chose to share their results were different from the overall applicant population.

With that limitation in mind, the comparisons are still interesting to discuss.

*The figures discussed here reflect College Intel's tally of 404 posts as of October 9, 2026. Its analysis may be updated as more results are collected.*

## Grades showed one of the clearest differences

One of the most noticeable findings involved unweighted GPA.

In College Intel's sample, decisions involving applicants with a 4.0 unweighted GPA had an admission rate of 38.2%, compared with 23.8% for those below 4.0.

That is a substantial difference within this particular group.

It suggests a strong association between academic performance and admission outcomes in the reported decisions.

But there's another part of the story.

Even applicants with a perfect GPA were rejected or waitlisted in approximately three out of every five decisions.

A 4.0 was not a guarantee.

And the comparison does not establish that having a 4.0, by itself, caused an applicant to be admitted.

For parents, I think this is a useful reminder.

Grades matter, but no single number tells the whole story of an application.

## What about SAT and ACT scores?

College Intel also found differences among applicants who submitted standardized test scores.

For SAT scores of 1550 or higher, the admission rate within the sample was 32.6%, compared with 23.6% for applicants who submitted lower SAT scores.

Applicants who submitted an ACT score of 36 had a 34.0% admission rate within the sample, compared with 25.6% for those submitting lower ACT scores.

Again, these are observed differences, not predictions for an individual student.

They also apply only to decisions where the college considered the test score. Test-blind institutions, such as UCLA and UC Berkeley, were excluded from those score comparisons.

And an interesting detail: as College Intel collected more posts, some of the differences changed considerably.

That tells us why small datasets deserve caution.

A pattern that looks impressive early on may become less dramatic as more information is collected.

## The extracurricular finding might surprise parents

This was one of the most interesting parts of the article.

We often hear that students need impressive extracurricular activities to stand out.

Research projects, varsity sports, community service, internships, and national awards can start to feel like requirements.

But across this particular sample, several common activities showed little difference in admission rates.

For example:

| Activity reported | With activity | Without activity |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Research | 29.0% | 28.4% |
| Varsity sport (not recruited) | 28.8% | 28.7% |
| Community service | 27.9% | 30.0% |

*Admission rates per decision within College Intel's self-selected sample, not official college admission rates or estimates of an applicant's chances. Source: [College Intel](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/accepted-vs-rejected-applicants-top-colleges-2026).*

Does that mean research, sports, and volunteering don't matter?

Not at all.

It means that, in this pooled dataset, simply reporting one of those activities was not strongly associated with a higher admission rate.

The analysis cannot fully account for the quality of the work, depth of commitment, essays, recommendations, intended major, or other important application factors.

College Intel also found that research was associated with different outcomes at certain individual universities.

So it would be a mistake to conclude that extracurriculars are unimportant everywhere.

For families, the more practical question might be whether a student is choosing activities because they're genuinely meaningful or because they're trying to assemble what they believe is a perfect application.

## Should students rush into summer programs or research?

The study found modest differences for applicants who reported selective summer programs and activities such as debate, speech, or Model United Nations.

But those differences do not prove that participating in a particular program increases a student's likelihood of admission.

This is especially important when families are considering expensive opportunities.

Before paying for a program, I would ask what the student will actually learn, whether the experience fits their interests, and whether a more affordable alternative could offer similar value.

A meaningful opportunity doesn't necessarily have to come with an impressive price tag.

And no program should be treated as a guaranteed path to admission.

## What the data cannot tell us

As useful as comparisons can be, this study has several limitations.

The information is self-reported and unverified. The people who post online are not randomly selected. Important application details may be missing, and one student may contribute decisions from many colleges.

That also means the 3,525 decisions are not 3,525 independent applicants. Students who reported more decisions contribute more observations to the pooled percentages.

The analysis also cannot fairly measure essays, recommendation letters, interviews, or the personal circumstances that admissions offices may consider.

And most importantly, these are associations in a selected group, not evidence that any single activity, grade, or test score caused admission.

That distinction matters.

We can learn from the patterns without turning them into a formula.

## What parents can take away

If I were using this information to guide a college-planning conversation, I would focus on three things.

First, help students build a strong academic foundation while recognizing that a perfect transcript does not guarantee admission.

Second, encourage activities that reflect genuine interests, commitment, and growth rather than trying to collect every possible credential.

And third, build a college list that includes a realistic range of options, considering academic fit, affordability, personal interests, and the support a student may need.

The [College Scorecard from the U.S. Department of Education](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/) is another helpful resource for exploring official college information and comparing options.

There is value in looking at admissions data.

But there is also value in remembering what the data cannot measure.

A student is more than a GPA, test score, activity list, or admission decision.

## A final thought for families

College admissions can make families feel as though every choice must be carefully calculated.

Which class will look better?

Which activity will stand out?

What score will be enough?

Research can help us ask better questions, but it cannot remove every uncertainty.

The goal isn't to find a guaranteed formula for getting into one particular university.

It's to help our children prepare thoughtfully, explore opportunities that fit them, and make decisions they can feel good about.

For the full analysis, including additional comparisons and important methodological limitations, read [College Intel's study of 3,525 reported 2026 admissions decisions](https://collegeintel.fuselyn.com/articles/accepted-vs-rejected-applicants-top-colleges-2026).

The numbers can help us understand part of the admissions picture.

They just shouldn't become the whole picture.
